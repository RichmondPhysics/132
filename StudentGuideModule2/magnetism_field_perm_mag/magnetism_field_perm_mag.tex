\textbf{Introduction} 

The \textit{magnetic field} around a permanent magnet is similar to an electric field around an arrangement of charged particles.  The direction of a magnetic field can be measured using a simple magnetic compass.  In this lab, you will map the magnetic field around a single magnet and around pairs of magnets in different orientations.

\textbf{Activity 1: A Single Bar Magnet}

\begin{enumerate}[labparts]

\item Place a single bar magnet on a piece of paper so that its south pole points north.\footnotemark ~
Trace the outline of the magnet, indicating its north and south poles.

\item Trace the field lines from the magnet as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item Place your small compass on the side of the magnet near the north pole
 and make a dot where the needle points.  (See figure)
 
\item Move your compass so that it points away from your first dot; make a second dot where the needle points to now.

%\hspace{0.05\textwidth}
%\includegraphics[trim={0 0 3.5mm 0},clip,width=0.94\textwidth]{magnetism_field_perm_mag/magnetism_2_fig_1.pdf}

\item Keep repeating, leap-frogging the compass over each previous dot until you reach either the edge of the magnet near 
its south pole or the edge of the paper.

\item Repeat the three steps above from different starting points near the magnet's north pole until you have an idea of the magnetic field over the entire paper.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Activity 2: Two Bar Magnets, Same Orientation}

\begin{enumerate}[labparts]
\item Place two bar magnets on a sheet of paper as shown to the right, about one fist-width apart. 

\item Repeat step (b) from Activity 1 to map out the field lines around both of these magnets.

\item What configuration of electric charges would produce an electric field that
looks similar to the magnetic field you just mapped out?
\answerspace{1 in}

\item What differences can you recognize between the magnetic field above and the analogous electric field?
\answerspace{30mm}
\end{enumerate}
